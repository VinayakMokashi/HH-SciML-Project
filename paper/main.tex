\begin{abstract}
Universal differential equations (UDEs) embed a neural network inside a
mechanistic model to learn a term the modeller does not know. In
conductance-based neuron models the natural application is to learn an unknown
ionic current from an observed voltage trace and then read the recovered
current's coefficients as biophysical estimates. We test whether that reading is
earned.
We replace the calcium current in a six-state Hodgkin--Huxley model with a
neural closure $U(V,s)$, train on the first \Ttrain\,ms of a single \Tfull\,ms
noisy trajectory, and
distil the closure back into the conductance form $s^2(aV+b)$.
% claim: ude-forecasts-accurately
% claim: voltage-only-equivalent
The UDE forecasts held-out voltage to $\valUdeForecastVRmseMeanStd$\,mV
(mean\,$\pm$\,SD over $\valUdeForecastVRmseN$ seeds) and holds the trajectory for
the entire remaining window; restricting the loss to voltage alone matches
full-state observation to within seed noise --- as it nearly must, since voltage
already carries \LossShareV\,\% of the unweighted full-state objective --- while
the five gate trajectories, which never enter the loss at all, are propagated
accurately by the retained kinetics.
% claim: physics-prior-reconstructs-unobserved-gates
% claim: symbolic-coefficients-not-identifiable
The conductance read off the closure, however, is only order-correct:
$\hat{a} = \valSymbolicAHatTrajTrainMeanStd$ against a true $\gcaMain$, with a
per-seed span of \valSymbolicAHatRangeTrajTrain{} that brackets the truth on both
sides.
% claim: parametric-fit-identifies-conductance
The usual explanation, that one trajectory cannot determine the conductance, is
wrong. Fitting the two calcium parameters directly to the \emph{same} trajectory,
with no network, returns $g_{Ca} = \valParamFitGcaHatTwoMeanStd$, with an
asymptotic relative standard error of $\valCondRelSeGcaTwoMean$. The
representative seed's own estimate is the lowest of the five, and the
profile-likelihood 95\% interval centred on \emph{that} estimate is $[1.6065, 2.1477]$; % numok: Delta-chi2 = 3.8415 crossings, linearly interpolated between the bracketing grid points of results/identifiability/profile_gca_gca2.0.csv
each realisation owns its own interval. % claim: closure-induced-nonidentifiability
The data determine the conductance to about six per cent under the correctly
weighted likelihood, where the estimate distilled from the closure scatters
roughly six times wider. Repeating that fit under the closure's own
voltage-dominated objective, changing nothing else, gives about twenty per cent,
so the objective and the representation each account for roughly half the gap;
the representation's own contribution is a factor of about two.
% claim: retrain-improves-loss-worsens-recovery
Nor is it under-training: quadrupling the budget lowers the training loss by
\LossDropPct\,\% while making every recovery quantity worse, including agreement with
the true trajectory inside the training window itself. What fails here is not parametric
identifiability but functional identifiability: the data confine the conductance
to a short, bounded stretch of the $g_{Ca}$--$E_{Ca}$ trade-off, and the estimates
read off the closure scatter well beyond it. Code, trained parameters, and all results tables are
available at \url{TODO-REPOSITORY-URL}; the archived snapshot and the licence
terms are given in the Data and Code Availability section.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Motivation: partially-known dynamics in conductance-based models}

Conductance-based neuron models are the workhorse of computational
neurophysiology, and they are almost never fully known. A modeller typically
has confident descriptions of the well-characterised currents (the fast sodium
and delayed-rectifier potassium currents of \citet{hodgkin1952}, plus a leak), and a
much weaker grip on the slower, modulatory currents that shape bursting and
adaptation. Universal differential equations
\citep{rackauckas2020universal}, mechanistic differential equations with a
neural network standing in for an unknown term, are an attractive way to close
that gap: keep the physics you trust, and learn the physics you do not from data.

The appeal is not only predictive. Because a UDE isolates the unknown term as a
single learned function, it invites a second step: distil that function back
into a closed form and read its coefficients as biophysical quantities. In a
conductance-based model those coefficients are a conductance and a reversal
potential. This two-stage recipe, fitting a network inside the ODE and then
running symbolic regression on it \citep{brunton2016sindy}, is exactly what a
practitioner reaching for interpretable mechanism would do, and it is what the
foundational UDE work proposes.

\subsection{The gap: from prediction to mechanism, and when it fails}

Reading a coefficient off a learned closure presumes that the data determine it.
That presumption is rarely tested. A UDE is usually judged by the diagnostics of
a predictive model: rollout error, a parity plot of the learned term against
ground truth. Both can look excellent while the coefficient the modeller
actually wants is not pinned down at all. Neuroscience has known for two decades
that conductance-based models are degenerate: very different parameter sets
produce indistinguishable activity \citep{prinz2004,marder2011}, and the gating
variables and conductances of a generalised Hodgkin--Huxley model are not
separately identifiable from voltage-clamp current recordings \citep{walch2016}, in which the potential is the experimenter's input rather than, as here, the free-running observable. That knowledge has not
been carried into the equation-discovery literature, which reports recovery
successes without asking whether recovery was possible in principle.

We study one instance of this gap in full. We take an advanced Hodgkin--Huxley
neuron (the classical model augmented with a persistent sodium current and a
high-threshold calcium current), hide the calcium current behind a neural
closure, and run the whole predict-then-interpret pipeline against ground truth
we control. The failure mode we find is the \emph{default practice}: fitting a
UDE to a single trajectory and reading off the learned current's coefficients is
exactly what a practitioner would reasonably do, and on this system it recovers
the current's shape while getting its scale wrong by a factor that varies across
random seeds.
% claim: closure-induced-nonidentifiability
The contribution is not that this can happen; it is a characterisation of
\emph{when} it happens, and a demonstration, through a chain of controls, that
the failure is a property of the flexible representation rather than of the data,
the fit, or the optimiser: on the same trajectory, a direct two-parameter fit
pins the conductance to about six per cent, while the estimates distilled from
the closure scatter across a factor of two and all but one of them fall outside
the short stretch of the trade-off the likelihood tolerates.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item % claim: ude-forecasts-accurately
  % claim: node-failure-is-architecture-not-budget
  \textbf{A reproducible advanced-HH SciML benchmark, with a baseline that is
  actually defensible.} Embedding the known physics and learning only the calcium
  current buys both accuracy and reliability: the UDE holds the \Ttube\,mV error
  tube for the entire \Rcap\,ms remainder on every seed, where a black-box neural
  ODE given the same budget and an autonomous architecture manages
  $\valNodeParityAutoRolloutMeanStd$\,ms. We reach that comparison by first
  repairing the baseline. Matching its optimisation budget to the UDE's changes
  nothing ($\valNodeParityTimeRolloutMeanStd$\,ms), while removing a raw time
  input from the right-hand side of an autonomous system improves it by more than
  an order of magnitude, so the failure is architectural
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-forecasting}, Table~\ref{tab:forecast}).

  \item % claim: voltage-only-equivalent
  % claim: physics-prior-reconstructs-unobserved-gates
  \textbf{A physics prior that propagates the unobserved state.} Observing
  \emph{only} voltage in the loss matches full-state observation to within seed
  noise --- as it nearly must: the objective is an unweighted sum in native
  units in which voltage already carries \LossShareV\,\% of the total, so the
  equivalence is close to arithmetic before any physics is invoked. The result
  that is \emph{not} near-tautological is what happens to the rest of the state:
  the five gate trajectories, which never enter the objective, are
  carried along accurately by the retained kinetics. This is propagation of
  unobserved states from a known initial condition, not reconstruction of them
  from voltage alone: the solver is started from the true full state and the
  exact gate kinetics are kept
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-partial}, Fig.~\ref{fig:voltage-only}).

  \item % claim: symbolic-form-recovered
  % claim: symbolic-coefficients-not-identifiable
  \textbf{Shape agrees, scale does not.} Given the conductance form, the distilled
  closure matches the true calcium current closely
  ($R^2 = \valSymbolicRTwoTrajTrainMeanStd$ on the supervised trajectory) while
  the conductance that same fit implies is only order-correct:
  $\hat{a} = \valSymbolicAHatTrajTrainMeanStd$ against a true value of $\gcaMain$,
  spanning \valSymbolicAHatRangeTrajTrain{} across seeds. Curve-fit quality and
  coefficient identifiability are different properties
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-symbolic}, Fig.~\ref{fig:symbolic}).

  \item % claim: parametric-fit-identifies-conductance
  % claim: closure-induced-nonidentifiability
  \textbf{The data are not the problem; the closure is.} Fitting the two calcium
  parameters directly to the \emph{same} trajectory, with no network, recovers
  $g_{Ca} = \valParamFitGcaHatTwoMeanStd$ against a true $\gcaMain$ (relative
  standard error $\valCondRelSeGcaTwoMean$, $\chi^2$ per point
  $\valParamFitChiSqPerPointTwo$). One trajectory identifies the conductance to
  about six per cent; the same conductance distilled from a $\NparamUde$-parameter
  closure scatters about six times wider. We do not attribute all of that to the
  representation, because the two arms also differ in objective: repeating the
  identical parametric fit under the closure's own voltage-dominated loss gives
  $\valParamFitGcaHatTwoSseFullMeanStd$, so the objective accounts for about a
  threefold inflation and the representation for a further twofold. This is
  parametric identifiability holding while functional identifiability fails
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-parametric}, Table~\ref{tab:parametric}).

  \item % claim: retrain-improves-loss-worsens-recovery
  \textbf{Not under-training, and we can now show the loss.} Quadrupling the
  optimisation budget \emph{lowers} the training objective, from
  $\valTrainLossBeforeMeanStd$ to $\valTrainLossAfterMeanStd$, while making every
  recovery quantity worse ($\hat{a} = \valRetrainAHatMeanStd$, hull domain). More optimisation
  buys a better fit to the noisy data and a worse model of the truth: even inside
  the training window, error against the clean trajectory rises
  ($\valUdeTrainVRmseMeanStd \to \valRetrainTrainVRmseMeanStd$\,mV). We use no
  validation split and no early stopping, so nothing in the protocol would have
  halted that descent
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-retrain}, Table~\ref{tab:retrain}).

  \item % claim: gca-sweep-shows-nonidentifiability
  \textbf{Recovery tracks signal amplitude, not fit quality.} Across a
  five-seed conductance sweep, normalised calcium error falls from
  $\valGcaSweepICaNormPointFourMeanStd$ at the physiological $g_{Ca}=\gcaPhys$ to
  $\valGcaSweepICaNormFourMeanStd$ at $g_{Ca}=\gcaHi$, while forecast voltage
  error is unchanged throughout
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-ablations}, Fig.~\ref{fig:gca}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Hybrid modelling: UDEs and neural ODEs.}
Neural ordinary differential equations parameterise a vector field with a network
\citep{chen2018neural}; universal differential equations generalise this by
placing a network inside an otherwise mechanistic model, keeping known structure
and learning only the unknown terms \citep{rackauckas2020universal}, an approach
adjacent to physics-informed neural networks \citep{raissi2019pinn}. The
technique is powerful, but its recovery guarantees are empirical rather than
theoretical, and the field is only now mapping where it fails.
\citet{silvestri2023analysis} analyse when UDEs recover the right dynamics, and
\citet{philipps2025ude} catalogue open problems: noise and limited data degrade
UDE performance, and explicit regularisation can improve both accuracy and
interpretability. This is
precisely the regime we probe.

\paragraph{From a learned function to a closed form.}
The second stage of the pipeline, distilling a learned function into an equation,
is standard: sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics
\citep{brunton2016sindy} and its variants \citep{rudy2017pdefind, zheng2019sr3,
fasel2022ensemblesindy, desilva2020pysindy}, and genetic or physics-inspired
symbolic regression \citep{cranmer2023pysr, udrescu2020aifeynman}. Every one of
these methods assumes the learned function is determined by the data. That
assumption is exactly what we test: we run sparse regression on a closure whose
predictions match ground truth well, and report what happens when the assumption
does not hold.

\paragraph{Conductance-based models and their currents.}
The advanced model we use is the squid-axon Hodgkin--Huxley set
\citep{hodgkin1952} in the modern minimal-model tradition \citep{pospischil2008},
augmented with a persistent sodium current (half-activation from
\citet{magistretti1999}; its conductance is our own choice, of the same order as
the models of \citet{golomb1997}, App.~\ref{app:eqns}) and a
high-threshold calcium current (reversal potential from
\citet{reuveni1993}; its activation curve is a logistic approximation to their kinetics, App.~\ref{app:eqns}). Every parameter we vary or hold fixed is either grounded in this
literature or declared as a modelling choice; the parameter table
(Sec.~\ref{sec:setup-model}) makes the provenance explicit, because the first
objection to any non-identifiability result is that the parameters were tuned to
produce it.

\paragraph{Degeneracy and identifiability.}
Two threads converge on our result. In neuroscience, disparate parameter sets
are long known to produce near-identical activity \citep{prinz2004},
model-averaging can fail outright \citep{golowasch2002}, families of
indistinguishable models are the rule rather than the exception
\citep{marder2011}, and the same holds under parametric variation of the
Hodgkin--Huxley model itself \citep{ori2018}. In systems identification,
structural and practical identifiability have precise definitions and diagnostics
\citep{raue2009, wieland2021}; \citet{walch2016} apply them to generalised
Hodgkin--Huxley models under voltage clamp --- where the potential is the imposed input and the membrane current the observable --- and prove that gating variables and conductances form
identifiable \emph{combinations} there rather than being separately identifiable, and
\citet{daly2015} reach a compatible conclusion by approximate Bayesian analysis.
Our voltage is instead a free-running state and the only observable, so those theorems are an analogue of our setting rather than a statement about it. Our result is nonetheless continuous with this body of work and imports it into the SciML
setting.

\paragraph{SciML applied to neurons, and the gap.}
Conductance-based neurons have a parameter- and state-estimation literature that uses no network at all: variational data assimilation \citep{meliza2014} and
classical prediction-error identification \citep{burghi2021feedback}. Separately, a domain-specific recurrent network has been used to recover hidden ionic states
\citep{iravanian2020}. Neural and universal differential equations enter later: UDEs have been
proposed as a unifying modelling language for the field \citep{elgazzar2025ude}.
On the Hodgkin--Huxley system specifically, \citet{kainth2025pinodesr} report
that standard neural ODEs and physics-informed variants forecast stiff
oscillatory dynamics unreliably under realistic iteration budgets, and stabilise
them by normalising physics residuals across state variables that evolve on
disparate timescales. That diagnosis is directly relevant to us twice over: it
independently corroborates the weakness of the black-box baseline we report in
Sec.~\ref{sec:results-forecasting}, and the imbalance it corrects is the same one
that makes our full-state and voltage-only conditions nearly identical
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results-partial}). Where they rebalance the residual to make the
learned vector field trainable, we hold the objective fixed and ask what the
resulting closure can be trusted to tell us about the physics.
Closest to our setting, \citet{beck2026hybrid} embed neural ODEs in
conductance-based models to learn unknown currents and misspecified kinetics.
By parameterising the network in terms of voltage-dependent steady-state and
time-constant functions they recover interpretable gating dynamics from voltage
recordings without assuming a functional form, validating across a channel
database of several thousand models that includes several hundred
calcium-channel models, and reducing a multicompartment cortical model to a
single compartment with a learned axial current. They own the claim that one can
put a network inside a Hodgkin--Huxley model and learn a hidden current, and we
do not contest it. They are also clear-eyed about the difficulty we study:
recovering gating dynamics from voltage alone is, in their words, inherently
underconstrained, because for a multi-gate channel different combinations of
steady-state and time-constant functions produce similar currents. Our
contribution is not to point that out but to \emph{measure} it in a case where
ground truth is controlled: which hidden current is recoverable, from how much of
a single trajectory, and whether an observed failure is optimisational or
structural. Two differences of scope are worth naming plainly, since they mark
where our experiments begin rather than any shortcoming of theirs: their
dedicated recovery demonstrations use sodium and potassium currents rather than
calcium, and they do not run symbolic regression, naming it instead as a
post-hoc step that could distil the learned curves into closed form. Distilling
the learned closure and asking what its coefficients are worth is exactly the
step we take.

The identifiability vocabulary we use comes from a parallel line of work.
\citet{loman2025ude} split UDE identifiability into a parametric and a functional
part, and report that inserting a network into a mechanistic ODE has limited
impact on the identifiability of the traditional parameters --- meaning the
mechanistic parameters that are \emph{retained} alongside the network, not the
term the network stands in for, which is where our non-identifiability lives;
\citet{loman2026structural} generalise structural parameter identifiability to
\emph{functional} identifiability and characterise model classes in which unique
recovery of an unknown function is impossible in principle, and
\citet{norden2025structural} measure the cost of ignoring it: machine-learning
models trained on partially observed dynamical systems generalise measurably
better when the system is first put into structurally identifiable coordinates,
so non-identifiability shows up as degraded generalisation and not only as a
caveat on interpretation. That distinction, between whether a parameter is
determined and whether a learned function is, organises what we report.

\section{Background and Problem Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{The advanced Hodgkin--Huxley model}
\label{sec:setup-model}

The system is a single-compartment neuron with state
$u = (V, m, h, n, p, s)$: membrane potential $V$, the classical sodium
activation/inactivation gates $m, h$, the potassium activation gate $n$, a
persistent-sodium gate $p$, and a calcium gate $s$. The membrane equation sums
five ionic currents against an applied drive,
\begin{equation}
C_m \frac{dV}{dt} = I_{\mathrm{app}} - I_K - I_{Na} - I_L - I_{NaP} - I_{Ca},
\qquad
I_{Ca} = g_{Ca}\, s^2\, (V - E_{Ca}),
\label{eq:membrane}
\end{equation}
with $I_K = g_K n^4 (V-E_K)$, $I_{Na} = g_{Na} m^3 h (V-E_{Na})$,
$I_L = g_L(V-E_L)$, and $I_{NaP} = g_{NaP}\, p\, (V-E_{Na})$. Each gate $x$ obeys
first-order kinetics $\dot{x} = \alpha_x(V)(1-x) - \beta_x(V)x$; the classical
$\alpha,\beta$ functions and the persistent-sodium and calcium steady-state
activations are given in Appendix~\ref{app:eqns}. The trajectory is a repetitive
spiking limit cycle driven by a constant $I_{\mathrm{app}}$.

The calcium current is the target of the study. It is a squared-gate current with
a linear driving force, so a conductance and a reversal potential are the two
biophysical quantities a successful recovery must return: in \eqref{eq:membrane},
$g_{Ca}$ is the conductance and $E_{Ca}$ the reversal potential, and the two
appear in the equivalent form $I_{Ca} = s^2(aV+b)$ with $a=g_{Ca}$ and
$b=-g_{Ca}E_{Ca}$.

Table~\ref{tab:params} lists every constant with its provenance. Sodium and
potassium kinetics and the passive parameters use the standard squid-axon values
\citep{hodgkin1952} in the modern resting-potential-referenced convention; the
persistent-sodium half-activation and the calcium reversal potential follow
\citet{magistretti1999} and \citet{reuveni1993}. The remaining values, including
the persistent-sodium conductance, are declared modelling choices, not
measurements. The calcium conductance $g_{Ca}$ is treated separately: it is not a
model constant but a builder argument and the axis of the identifiability
experiment of Sec.~\ref{sec:results-ablations}, set to $\gcaMain$ for the
headline runs and swept down to the physiological $\gcaPhys$; we return to that
choice below and in Sec.~\ref{sec:setup-hidden}.

\subsection{Known physics versus the hidden current: the recovery task}
\label{sec:setup-hidden}

The recovery task is deliberately favourable to the method. All four other
currents and all five gate kinetics, including the calcium gate's own dynamics
$\dot s$, are retained exactly; only the algebraic term $I_{Ca}$ in
\eqref{eq:membrane} is unknown and must be learned as a function of $(V,s)$. The
gate $s$ is therefore available to the closure as an input because \emph{its
kinetics are known physics}, not because calcium is observed. The two unknowns
are the conductance $g_{Ca}$ and the reversal potential $E_{Ca}$; the functional
form $s^2(V-E_{Ca})$ is what the symbolic stage is asked to rediscover.

The physiological reference value deserves its own sentence, because everything
below is calibrated against it. \citet{pospischil2008} model the same
\citet{reuveni1993} high-threshold calcium current in an intrinsically-bursting
cortical pyramidal cell at a density of \gcaLit\,\nicefrac{mS}{cm$^2$} alongside a
sodium density of \gNaLit\,\nicefrac{mS}{cm$^2$} (their published implementation,
ModelDB accession 123623). % numok: literature densities, demo_PY_IB.hoc:133,137
Our model inherits the classical squid-axon $g_{Na}=\mgNa$\,\nicefrac{mS}{cm$^2$}
rather than their cortical value, so the quantity to carry across is the
\emph{ratio} of calcium to sodium conductance, not the absolute density. Holding
that ratio fixed gives
$(\gcaLit/\gNaLit)\times\mgNa = \gcaRescaled$\,\nicefrac{mS}{cm$^2$}, which we
round to $\gcaPhys$ and call physiological throughout; their
bursting-repetitive variant maps to $\gcaRescaledIBR$ on the same rescaling.
So $\gcaPhys$ is a literature-derived quantity with a stated derivation, not a
round number chosen to make a negative result come out.

% claim: gca-two-is-a-controlled-choice
The main experiments use $g_{Ca}=\gcaMain$, five times that physiological value.
This is a deliberate choice, not a convenience, and we defend
it as an experiment rather than a setting: $g_{Ca}$ controls how much the calcium
current contributes to the voltage the loss can see, so it \emph{is} an
identifiability axis. We sweep it in full (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-ablations}),
report the value where the method fails hardest, and make that failure part of
the result. At the physiological value the calcium current contributes so little
to the voltage that the \emph{closure} cannot recover it at all --- though a
direct two-parameter fit to the same trajectory still can
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results-parametric}), so this is a limit of the closure and not of
the data. Setting $g_{Ca}=\gcaMain$ moves the problem into the regime where
recovery through the closure is possible enough to be interesting, which is
exactly why the residual coefficient gap there is a finding rather than a shrug.

\subsection{Data generation and evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:setup-data}

For each run we solve the true model over $[0,\Tfull]$\,ms, sample $\Ndata$
points, and add Ornstein--Uhlenbeck noise to every channel, each channel's noise
path renormalised to unit variance and then scaled to \NoisePct\,\% of that
channel's own standard deviation. That percentage is the noise magnitude
throughout, and because the scaling is per channel it is the same \emph{relative}
perturbation everywhere: in absolute terms the baseline corresponds to about
$\sigmaV$\,mV on voltage and $\sigmaGateLo$--$\sigmaGateHi$ on the dimensionless
gates.
The process is nominally temporally correlated, but at this sampling interval it
is not: with $\theta=\mOUth$\,ms$^{-1}$ and $\Delta t \approx \dtSample$\,ms the
discrete relaxation factor $1-\theta\Delta t$ is about $\ouAR$, so successive
samples are effectively independent. (The path is generated by a single
Euler--Maruyama step per sample, so $1-\theta\Delta t$ is literally the discrete
autocorrelation; an exactly integrated Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process would instead
give $e^{-\theta\Delta t}\approx0.38$.)
We state it because ``temporally correlated noise'' would
otherwise overstate what the data actually contain. Models are trained on the noisy
data over $t\le\Ttrain$\,ms and evaluated against the \emph{clean} trajectory, so
error measures recovery of the underlying dynamics rather than memorisation of
noise; a secondary error against the noisy target records the noise floor. The
forecast window is $t>\Ttrain$\,ms of a single uninterrupted integration from the
true initial condition; the model is never re-initialised at the train/forecast
boundary. For the training-window ablation, where the forecast window would
otherwise move with the training length, every length is additionally scored on a
fixed \emph{common} window $t>\Tcommon$\,ms that is unseen for all of them.

\subsection{Black-box Neural ODE baseline}

The baseline is a black-box neural ODE \citep{chen2018neural} that replaces the
entire right-hand side with a network of the six normalised states and time,
trained on the same data. It carries no physics and serves only to establish the
scale of the forecasting problem: what ``the UDE extrapolates'' means depends on
what a model without the physics prior does on the same task. Architecture and
budgets are in Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}.

\subsection{Universal differential equation with embedded physics}

The UDE is byte-identical to the true right-hand side except for one line: the
algebraic term $I_{Ca}=g_{Ca}s^2(V-E_{Ca})$ is replaced by
$U(V,s)=\mathrm{net}(\NNinputs;\theta)$, a small multilayer perceptron
(two hidden layers of width sixteen, $\NparamUde$ parameters). Everything
else (the four known currents, all five gate ODEs, $I_{\mathrm{app}}$, $C_m$, the
reversal potentials) is the exact physics. The network never sees $g_{Ca}$; that
constant shapes only the true data being fitted.

The observation condition is a one-line change to the loss. Writing
$u(t)=(V,m,h,n,p,s)$ for the noisy data and $\hat u(t;\theta)$ for the solver's
output, the two objectives are
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{full}}(\theta)
    = \sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{train}}}\ \sum_{i=1}^{6}
      \bigl(u_i(t)-\hat u_i(t;\theta)\bigr)^2,
  \qquad
  \mathcal{L}_{V}(\theta)
    = \sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{train}}}
      \bigl(V(t)-\hat V(t;\theta)\bigr)^2 .
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}

There is no normalisation and there are no channel weights: the sum is taken in
the states' native units, with $V$ in millivolts and the five gates
dimensionless on $[0,1]$. That is worth being explicit about, because it means
the six channels are nowhere near equally weighted in practice. Each channel's
residual scales with its own noise magnitude, which is \NoisePct\,\% of its own
standard deviation, and the voltage trace's standard deviation exceeds the mean
gate standard deviation by more than $\GateSdRatio\times$; at the noise floor the
voltage term therefore carries \LossShareV\,\% of the expected value of
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{full}}$. In other words $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{full}}$ is
already almost $\mathcal{L}_{V}$, and the full-state and voltage-only conditions
are far closer to each other than the phrase ``six observed channels versus one''
suggests. We return to this when interpreting their measured equivalence
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results-partial}); it is the explanation, not a caveat discovered
afterwards.

The black-box neural ODE is the one model that does not use \eqref{eq:loss}
verbatim: it trains in normalised coordinates, with the voltage row divided by
$\NodeScaleV$. That division cuts the voltage residual's contribution by a factor
of $\NodeScaleV^{2}$, which does more than rescale the objective---it inverts the
channel weighting. Where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{full}}$ is almost all voltage
(\LossShareV\,\%), the neural ODE's objective at the noise floor is dominated
instead by the five gate channels, which carry roughly three quarters of it. So
its loss values are not numerically comparable to the UDE's and, more to the
point, the two optimisers are not solving the same weighted problem: one spends
essentially all of its gradient on the voltage trace and the other spends most of
its gradient on the gates. Within each model family the losses are directly
comparable.

In both conditions the solver still integrates all six states from the full true
initial condition, so ``voltage only'' refers to observation in the loss, not to
what is known about the initial state or evolved by the physics.

\subsection{Symbolic recovery of the learned current}
\label{sec:methods-symbolic}

Given a trained closure $U$, we recover an equation for it in two complementary
ways, both fit on a grid of $(V,s)$ points restricted to the convex hull of the
trajectory's $(V,s)$ cloud, taken over the full simulated span rather than the
training window alone. We chose that restriction because the
spiking limit cycle is a near-one-dimensional loop in $(V,s)$, which we expected
to leave the terms poorly separable on the trajectory itself, whereas the
hull-masked grid is the network's genuine two-dimensional interpolation region.
We report in Sec.~\ref{sec:results-symbolic} that this reasoning does not
survive measurement for the two-term conductance form, which is well conditioned
on both domains; we did not measure it for the six-term library, so the
restriction is a design choice we made in advance rather than one the data
compelled.

The first recovery is unconstrained sparse regression over the low-order library
$\{1, V, s, Vs, s^2, Vs^2\}$, which contains the truth exactly, by standardised
sequentially-thresholded least squares followed by ordinary-least-squares
debiasing, in the manner of SINDy \citep{brunton2016sindy}. The constant term is
held out of the standardised thresholding step and always enters the active set,
so the sparsest model this estimator can return keeps five of the six terms. The second is a
structure-informed fit to the biophysical conductance form $s^2(aV+b)$, whose
coefficients map directly to the physics: $a=g_{Ca}$, $b=-g_{Ca}E_{Ca}$, and the
reversal potential is $-b/a$. As a wiring check we apply the identical
estimator, on the identical hull-restricted domain, to the \emph{analytic} true
current. That check is exact by construction---the analytic current lies in the
span of the estimator's two columns, so least squares returns the true pair to
machine precision on any domain that leaves the design matrix full rank---so it
confirms that the estimator, the hull-restricted domain and the mask are the ones
we describe, and cannot detect a bias. Recovered forms are
scored against the true current on the trajectory.

\subsection{A parametric reference: what the data alone determine}
\label{sec:methods-parametric}

The symbolic stage asks what a \emph{closure} says the conductance is. To know
what that answer is worth we need the other one: what the \emph{data} say the
conductance is. So we also solve the ordinary parameter-estimation problem on
exactly the same trajectory, with the true structure known and no network
anywhere. Only $g_{Ca}$ and $E_{Ca}$ are free; every other constant, the initial
condition, the training window and the solver are those of the UDE runs, and so
is the tolerance $\Tol$ used for the $\chi^2$ fit itself; the finite-difference
sensitivity solves behind the Fisher diagnostics are run tighter, at
$\TolFisher$, so that the parameter perturbation and not the integrator sets the
difference error. Two things are deliberately \emph{not} shared, and we name them here
rather than let a reader find them: the objective and the optimiser.

The parametric arm needs a weighted objective rather than the raw sum of squares
of \eqref{eq:loss}, because only a weighted objective is a likelihood and two of
the three quantities we report are likelihood diagnostics. We use
\begin{equation}
  \chi^2(g_{Ca}, E_{Ca})
    = \sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{train}}}\ \sum_{i=1}^{6}
      \frac{\bigl(u_i(t)-\hat u_i(t)\bigr)^2}{\sigma_i^2},
  \qquad
  \sigma_i = \NoisePct\,\%\times \mathrm{SD}(u_i).
  \label{eq:chisq}
\end{equation}
The sums run over all six channels at each of the \Ntrain{} samples in the
training window, so the $\chi^2$ per data point quoted later divides by
$\Nchisq$ residuals. Here $\sigma_i$ is the \emph{known} noise magnitude of each channel, since we
generated it. Minimisation is by multi-start Nelder--Mead from three starting
points spread across the plausible range, none of which is the true pair, and
every quantity below, the point estimate included, is taken from that
minimisation. Only the best of the three minima is kept and the optimiser's
convergence flag is not recorded, so the multi-start is a robustness precaution
rather than an audited agreement check.

This is where the two arms part company beyond the representation. The UDE
descends \eqref{eq:loss}, unweighted and in the states' native units, by Adam
followed by a BFGS polish under a continuous adjoint; the parametric arm
descends \eqref{eq:chisq}, in which every channel is divided by its own noise
scale, by a derivative-free simplex method. Since \eqref{eq:loss} is
\LossShareV\,\% voltage at the noise floor while \eqref{eq:chisq} weighs the six
channels comparably, the two arms are not putting the same question to the data.
Representation, objective and optimiser therefore change together between them,
so we separate them rather than assume they do not matter: we repeat the
\emph{identical} fit --- same data, same true functional form, same two free
parameters, same three starting points, same solver and tolerances --- under
\eqref{eq:loss} instead of \eqref{eq:chisq}. That control is reported in
Sec.~\ref{sec:results-parametric} and it is what licenses reading any part of the
gap as representation. Three diagnostics follow:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The estimate and its seed spread.} The fit is repeated on all
  $\valUdeForecastVRmseN$ seeds, giving a spread to set beside the one the
  symbolic stage produces.
  \item \textbf{Profile likelihood.} Fixing $g_{Ca}$ on a grid and re-optimising
  $E_{Ca}$ at each point (and the mirror image for $E_{Ca}$) traces the
  likelihood along each axis. The 95\% interval is where the profile stays % numok: 95% confidence level, a convention
  within $\chi^2_{\min}+3.84$, the standard diagnostic of % numok: chi-square 95% quantile, 1 dof
  \citet{raue2009}. An interval that runs to the edge of the grid is the
  signature of a parameter the data do not bound.
  \item \textbf{Conditioning.} We form the Fisher information from first-order
  sensitivities, $F_{ij} = \sum_{k,t}\sigma_k^{-2}\,\partial_i \hat u_{kt}\,
  \partial_j \hat u_{kt}$, and read relative standard errors and eigen-directions
  off it.
\end{itemize}

Two choices there are load-bearing enough to state in the main text. The
information matrix is built from \emph{first-order} sensitivities rather than
from a finite-difference Hessian of $\chi^2$: this is a spiking model, a small
parameter change shifts spike phase, and $\chi^2$ consequently rises by orders of
magnitude within a fraction of a per cent of the optimum, so its second
differences are dominated by that hyper-curvature at any usable step size. And
the information is expressed in \emph{log} parameters, because $g_{Ca}\approx 2$
and $E_{Ca}\approx\mECa$ carry incommensurate units and magnitudes; in raw units
the least-informative eigenvector falls on a coordinate axis as an artifact of
units rather than a finding about data.

We stress what this comparison can and cannot license. A two-parameter model
handed the true functional form should beat a $\NparamUde$-parameter closure
handed no form at all, and it does, including on forecast error. The inference we
draw is one-directional and does not need more: if the data pin the conductance,
then the closure's failure to pin it cannot be attributed to the data.

\subsection{Training, metrics, and reproducibility}
\label{sec:methods-train}

% claim: forecast-is-one-uninterrupted-rollout
All models are integrated with an explicit Runge--Kutta method
\citep{rackauckas2017diffeq} at tight tolerances, trained by Adam followed by a
BFGS polish under a continuous adjoint \citep{ma2021comparison,
rackauckas2020universal}, with the network parameterised explicitly
\citep{pal2023lux} and the optimisation driven through a unified interface
\citep{dixit2023optimization}; exact budgets are in Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}.
Every model is trained to a fixed iteration budget: we hold out no validation
split, apply no early-stopping criterion and no explicit regularisation, and
always report the final iterate. Nothing in the protocol therefore detects or
prevents fitting the particular noise realisation, which is the caveat the
aggressive-retrain control of Sec.~\ref{sec:results-retrain} must be read with.
Forecast error is the tail of a single continuous integration from the true
initial condition (extrapolation error, not one-step-ahead error), because the
model is never re-initialised at the boundary.

Every headline metric is a mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation over
$\valUdeForecastVRmseN$ seeds. The conductance sweep is likewise run over all
$\valUdeForecastVRmseN$ seeds (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-ablations}); the remaining
ablations are single runs at the pre-registered baseline seed. A seed jointly redraws \emph{both} the network initialisation and
the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck noise realisation, so the reported spread is a joint
init-and-data spread and the two sources cannot be separated from these runs; we
report it as such and run no statistical test.
% claim: rep-seed-is-guarded-not-cherry-picked
The single seed used for the trajectory and parity panels is the pre-registered
baseline, fixed before the seed spread was known, and a guardrail confirms it is
median-typical (within the inter-quartile range of the five-seed
distribution) rather than selected from it.

% claim: calcium-error-is-decoupled-from-trajectory-error
Two error families are reported and must not be conflated. Voltage error
accumulates over a free rollout. The calcium-current error probes the learned
closure at the true clean $(V,s)$ and is therefore a function-approximation
error, decoupled from trajectory error; a normalised version divides it by the
true current's standard deviation within the window, so a value above one means
the closure's error exceeds the signal it is meant to reproduce. It is precisely
because these two are separable that ``accurate voltage, wrong coefficients'' is
coherent rather than contradictory.

\subsection{Forecasting: the physics prior buys accuracy and reliability}
\label{sec:results-forecasting}

Both models are trained on $t\le\Ttrain$\,ms of one noisy trajectory and then
integrated freely to \Tfull\,ms from the true initial condition; the forecast
window is the tail of one uninterrupted rollout.

% claim: ude-forecasts-accurately
The UDE never breaches the \Ttube\,mV tube (Fig.~\ref{fig:ude-overview}). Its
rollout horizon saturates the
metric's cap for every seed ($\valUdeForecastRollout$\,ms; the cap is \Rcap\,ms,
essentially the whole remaining trajectory, so the horizon is right-censored and
its true value is unknown), and its forecast voltage RMSE is
$\valUdeForecastVRmseMeanStd$\,mV.
% claim: ude-learns-true-dynamics-not-noise
The fit is not memorisation of the training window (Fig.~\ref{fig:bars}): the
UDE's training-window error against the clean trajectory,
$\valUdeTrainVRmseMeanStd$\,mV, is well below its error against the noisy signal
it was actually fit to, $\valUdeTrainVRmseVsNoisyMeanStd$\,mV. The inequality on its own is weak, since
it holds for any model that does not interpolate the individual noisy samples;
what carries the argument is the \emph{level} of the second number. The voltage
channel was corrupted with additive noise of standard deviation \NoisePct\,\% of
that channel's own SD (the $\sigma_i$ of \eqref{eq:chisq}), so a model that had
recovered the clean signal exactly would score approximately that noise level
against the noisy target rather than zero. The UDE scores at that level, and
that is what licenses reading the training-window error as recovery of the
underlying dynamics rather than of the noise.

\paragraph{Repairing the baseline before using it.}
% claim: node-failure-is-architecture-not-budget
A black-box neural ODE is only worth comparing against if it has been given a
fair chance, and our first one had not. As initially configured it trained for
fewer Adam iterations than the UDE, and it took \emph{raw time} as an input
alongside the six states. The second of those is the more serious: the true
system is autonomous, driven by a constant $I_{\mathrm{app}}$, so a right-hand
side that reads the clock is strictly more expressive than the physics requires,
and it can fit the training window by time index instead of learning a vector
field. Figure~\ref{fig:node-vs-ude} shows that failure for the published
configuration. We therefore ran two controls, each over
$\valUdeForecastVRmseN$ seeds, and they separate cleanly
(Table~\ref{tab:forecast}).

Raising the budget to the UDE's own \AdamUde/\BfgsUde{} while keeping the
architecture does not help. The rollout horizon does not improve, it drifts down
($\valNodeForecastRolloutMeanStd \to \valNodeParityTimeRolloutMeanStd$\,ms), and
the forecast error does not fall. We give that error as a span rather than a
mean, because both time-indexed configurations blow up rather than degrade and a
standard deviation over blown-up solves is not an error bar: the published
configuration lands between $\valNodePublishedVRmseMin$ and
$\valNodePublishedVRmseMax$\,mV across seeds, the parity-budget one between
$\valNodeParityTimeVRmseMin$ and $\valNodeParityTimeVRmseMax$\,mV. Whatever is
wrong is not a shortage of optimisation, and more of it does not rescue the
forecast, consistent with buying a tighter fit of the training window by clock
index.

Removing the time input at that same budget changes the picture entirely. The
horizon goes to $\valNodeParityAutoRolloutMeanStd$\,ms, roughly forty-five times
the time-indexed variant, and the median forecast error falls to
$\valNodeParityAutoVRmseMedian$\,mV. Four of the five seeds reproduce the spike
count \emph{exactly}. So the catastrophic failure of the original baseline was an
artifact of an architecture nobody should have used on an autonomous system, and
we report the repaired one instead.

The honest comparison that survives has two parts, and neither is the categorical
claim we could have made from the broken baseline. The first is magnitude: the
UDE's $\valUdeForecastVRmseMeanStd$\,mV against a median
$\valNodeParityAutoVRmseMedian$\,mV, about seventy-fold. Both figures are
forecast RMSE against the clean trajectory and are scored identically, so that
comparison is like-for-like; what is \emph{not} comparable is the two models'
training objectives, since the neural ODE optimises normalised coordinates in
which the gate channels rather than the voltage carry most of the residual
(see the discussion following \eqref{eq:loss}). The second, and we think
the more telling, is reliability. The UDE saturates the \Rcap\,ms cap on every
seed --- the horizon is right-censored there, so it has no spread left to
report --- and its forecast voltage error varies across seeds by only
$\pm\valUdeForecastVRmseStd$\,mV; the autonomous
neural ODE's horizon ranges from $5.5$ to $44.4$\,ms % numok: per-seed rollouts, results/node_parity/node_parity_summary.csv
and one seed in five still fails outright. A physics prior does not merely make
the average forecast better here, it makes the forecast dependable, and that
difference is invisible in any single run. What this establishes remains narrow:
it does not establish that the learned term \emph{is} the calcium current, which
is the subject of Sec.~\ref{sec:results-symbolic}.

\subsection{Partial observation: the voltage-only UDE}
\label{sec:results-partial}

% claim: physics-prior-reconstructs-unobserved-gates
Restricting the loss to the voltage trace alone removes the five gate variables
from the objective entirely; they are never observed, never scored, and never
supervised. They are nonetheless tracked to a mean gate RMSE of
$\valUdeVoltageForecastGateRmseMeanStd$ over the forecast window. Nothing in the
network learned them; the retained gate kinetics did, once the learned
$I_{Ca}(V,s)$ was constrained enough by the voltage fit to keep the vector field
on the manifold.

We are deliberate about what this does and does not show, because the natural
reading overstates it. The gates are \emph{propagated}, not \emph{inferred}. The
solver is initialised from the true six-dimensional state, and the exact gate
kinetics $\dot x = \alpha_x(V)(1-x)-\beta_x(V)x$ are retained as known physics,
so once $V(t)$ is right the gates are determined by integrating known equations
from a known start. Nothing here recovers the gates from voltage alone, and
nothing here would survive an unknown initial condition. What the result does
establish is narrower and still worth having: the voltage residual alone
constrains the learned closure tightly enough that the whole six-dimensional
vector field stays on the correct manifold for the entire un-trained remainder,
without the gates ever appearing in the objective
(Fig.~\ref{fig:voltage-only}).

% claim: voltage-only-equivalent
Restricting the loss to voltage also matches full-state observation to within
seed noise on the quantities that can be compared: forecast $V$ RMSE
$\valUdeVoltageForecastVRmseMeanStd$ versus $\valUdeForecastVRmseMeanStd$\,mV, and
forecast $I_{Ca}$ RMSE $\valUdeVoltageForecastICaRmseMeanStd$ versus
$\valUdeForecastICaRmseMeanStd$\,\nicefrac{$\mu$A}{cm$^2$}
($n=\valUdeForecastVRmseN$; no significance test is possible at this sample size
and none is claimed). We do not rank the two conditions: the differences are well
inside the seed spread, and the finding of interest is the equivalence itself.

The equivalence is also less mysterious than it first appears, and the loss
normalisation of \eqref{eq:loss} says why. Because the objective is an unweighted
sum in native units, the five gate terms together contribute only about
$\LossShareGates$\,\% of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{full}}$ at the noise floor.
Dropping them is therefore a small perturbation of the objective, not the removal
of five sixths of the supervision, and the two conditions are close to each other
by construction before any physics is invoked.
% claim: voltage-only-common-eval-matches-full
The equivalence also survives on an interval that is unseen for \emph{both}
conditions rather than each model's own forecast window: on the shared window
$t>\Tcommon$\,ms full-state observation gives $\valCommonEvalVRmseFull$\,mV
against voltage-only's $\valCommonEvalVRmseVoltage$\,mV, and
$\valCommonEvalICaRmseFull$ against $\valCommonEvalICaRmseVoltage$ on the
calcium current. What the physics prior supplies is
the part that follows: the gates are then propagated correctly from the true
initial condition by the retained kinetics. Reporting the equivalence without
reporting the weighting would invite the stronger and unsupported reading that
five observed channels were worth nothing.

\subsection{Ablations: calcium identifiability, training window, and noise}
\label{sec:results-ablations}

% claim: gca-sweep-shows-nonidentifiability
The conductance sweep asks what governs recovery through the closure, and it is
now run over all $\valUdeForecastVRmseN$ seeds at every point rather than one
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gca}). Sweeping
$g_{Ca}\in\{\gcaPhys,\gcaMid,\gcaMain,\gcaHi\}$ with every other axis pinned, the
normalised calcium error falls from $\valGcaSweepICaNormPointFourMeanStd$ through
$\valGcaSweepICaNormOneMeanStd$ and $\valGcaSweepICaNormTwoMeanStd$ to
$\valGcaSweepICaNormFourMeanStd$. % numok: gCa sweep axis values, experiments_runner.jl:46
Signal amplitude is the only thing that changes across this axis (same
architecture, same data volume, same optimiser, same iterations), so it is an
identifiability axis by construction, and forecast voltage error is flat along it
($\valGcaSweepVRmsePointFourMeanStd$\,mV at $\gcaPhys$ against
$\valUdeForecastVRmseMeanStd$\,mV at $\gcaMain$). How well the hidden term is
recovered is governed by how much it moves the observed channel, not by how well
the observed channel is fitted.

We claim the end-to-end trend and not the individual steps, because with error
bars only one step survives. The overall eightfold fall from $\gcaPhys$ to
$\gcaHi$ is far outside seed noise, but of the three adjacent comparisons only
$\gcaMid \to \gcaMain$ clears two pooled standard deviations; $\gcaPhys \to
\gcaMid$ and $\gcaMain \to \gcaHi$ do not. % numok: 2.4, 1.8 and 1.3 pooled SD, scripts/summarize_gca_sweep.py
The seed spread is itself strongly conductance-dependent,
$\pm\valGcaSweepICaNormPointFourStd$ at $\gcaPhys$ against
$\pm\valGcaSweepICaNormTwoStd$ at $\gcaMain$, a factor of thirteen, so each point
must be judged against its own spread rather than the centre point's.

A single-seed version of this sweep would have told a tidier and less accurate
story. At $g_{Ca}=\gcaPhys$ our representative seed gives
$\valGcaAblICaNormPointFour$, above the
threshold at which the closure's error would exceed the calcium current's own
variation; the five-seed mean is $\valGcaSweepICaNormPointFourMean$ and only one
seed in five exceeds that threshold. % numok: per-seed values, results/gca_sweep_5seed/gca_sweep_multiseed.csv
So the honest statement is that recovery at the physiological conductance
approaches the point of uselessness rather than passing it.

The five seeds also expose a mechanism the normalised metric hides. The
\emph{raw} calcium error is statistically flat across the whole sweep
($\valGcaSweepICaRawPointFourMeanStd$ at $\gcaPhys$ against
$\valGcaSweepICaRawTwoMeanStd$ at $\gcaMain$, intervals overlapping): the
closure's absolute error barely moves, and what changes is the size of the signal
it is being asked to explain. Finally, at $g_{Ca}=\gcaPhys$ the symbolic fit
returns the \emph{wrong sign} for the conductance
($\hat a = \valSymbolicAHatNegControl$ against a true $+\gcaPhys$; hull domain,
single seed --- on the supervised trajectory the estimate is instead
indistinguishable from zero, Sec.~\ref{sec:results-retrain}), where the
parametric fit on the same data returns
$\valParamFitGcaHatPointFourMeanStd$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-parametric}).

% claim: window-needs-sufficient-length
The training-window sweep, scored on the shared unseen window
$t>\Tcommon$\,ms, shows that too little data fails: a fifteen-millisecond window
gives $\valWindowAblCommonEvalVRmseFifteen$\,mV, roughly seven times the value at
the twenty- and thirty-millisecond windows and at least three and a half times
the value at every longer one. Beyond twenty milliseconds the
axis carries no evidence that more data helps. The twenty- and thirty-millisecond
windows are indistinguishable ($\valWindowAblCommonEvalVRmseTwenty$ and
$\valWindowAblCommonEvalVRmseThirty$\,mV, a gap well inside the
$\pm\valUdeForecastVRmseStd$\,mV five-seed spread at the centre point), while the
forty- and fifty-millisecond windows are measurably \emph{worse}
($\valWindowAblCommonEvalVRmseForty$ and $\valWindowAblCommonEvalVRmseFifty$\,mV,
several times that spread; single seed per point). That rise is \emph{not}
evidence that longer windows are intrinsically harder: every window trains for
the same fixed budget regardless of how many points it must fit, so the longer
windows are under-optimised, and the axis confounds window length with
optimisation adequacy. We read it only as ``a sufficiently long window is
required, and twenty milliseconds suffices,'' and draw no conclusion from the
non-monotonicity beyond naming its likely cause
(Appendix~\ref{app:extra}, Fig.~\ref{fig:appendix}).

% claim: noise-ablation-degrades-gracefully
The noise-level sweep completes the ablation set. Holding every other axis at
baseline and varying the observation-noise level over $\{0, 1, 2, 5, 10\}$ per cent of % numok: noise sweep axis, experiments_runner.jl:44; the 2 per cent baseline point is B_NOISE, experiments_runner.jl:49
each channel's own standard deviation, the normalised calcium error rises
monotonically: $\valNoiseAblICaNormZero$, $\valNoiseAblICaNormOnePct$,
$\valNoiseAblICaNormTwoPct$, $\valNoiseAblICaNormFivePct$,
$\valNoiseAblICaNormTenPct$. Two readings follow. The zero-noise point is a
control the paper otherwise lacks: with no observation noise at all the closure's
calcium error does not vanish, it falls only to about a tenth of its value at the
baseline level, so part of what the closure gets wrong is its own approximation
error rather than the data's noise. And the degradation is gentle up to the
baseline level and steep only beyond it, which puts the \NoisePct\,\% setting
used everywhere else on the flat part of that curve rather than at a cliff edge.
Two caveats travel with the axis. It is a single run per point at the
pre-registered baseline seed, like the window sweep, and carries no error bars.
And the symbolic distillation was not repeated at any noise level other than the
baseline, so nothing here speaks directly to coefficient recovery
(Appendix~\ref{app:extra}, Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}).

\subsection{Symbolic recovery of the calcium current}
\label{sec:results-symbolic}

% claim: symbolic-form-recovered
Constrained to the biophysical conductance form $s^2(aV+b)$, the distilled
closure reproduces the true calcium current with
$R^2 = \valSymbolicRTwoTrajTrainMeanStd$ against the analytic ground truth on the
supervised trajectory ($n=\valUdeForecastVRmseN$, per-seed mean $\pm$ SD;
Fig.~\ref{fig:parity}). We are deliberate about what that does and does not
say. The form $s^2(aV+b)$ was \emph{supplied} to the estimator, not found by it,
so this is agreement with an assumed form rather than discovery of one. That
number also scores the fit against the \emph{truth}, while the statement we want
is about the \emph{closure}: whether the two-term form describes what the network
actually learned. Scored against the network's own output on the supervised
trajectory it does, at $R^2 = \valSymbolicRTwoNnTrajTrainMeanStd$, with per-seed
values spanning \valSymbolicRTwoNnRangeTrajTrain{}. On the wider convex hull, the
domain the two symbolic figure panels are fitted on, the same agreement is looser
and far more seed-dependent ($\valSymbolicRTwoNnHullMeanStd$ on the hull,
spanning \valSymbolicRTwoNnRangeHull{}), so the expected conductance form
describes the closure well where the closure was supervised and less well where
it extrapolates. The discovery question is answered separately, and negatively,
below.

% claim: symbolic-coefficients-not-identifiable
The \emph{coefficients} that same fit implies are another matter. The fitted
conductance is $\hat a = \valSymbolicAHatTrajTrainMeanStd$ against a true value of
$\gcaMain$, with a standard deviation about two-fifths of the mean, and the
per-seed estimates span \valSymbolicAHatRangeTrajTrain{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:symbolic}).
On the wider convex-hull domain used below for the unconstrained comparison the
picture is the same or slightly worse ($\valSymbolicAHatMeanStd$, spanning
\valSymbolicAHatRange), while the offset $\hat b = \valSymbolicBHatMeanStd$ is the
better determined of the two, scattering by about a quarter of its own magnitude
% numok: b rel. spread 22.9% vs a 56.7% on the hull, results/identifiability/symbolic_domain_comparison.csv
against the conductance's half. These are five different closures that happen
to fit the same voltage, not five measurements of one conductance. A curve can match the
truth at $R^2$ near unity while the coefficient it implies is off by a fifth with
a two-fifths spread; curve-fit quality and coefficient identifiability are
different properties, and this is the cleanest place in the study to see it.

% claim: symbolic-domain-robustness
The domain the fit is evaluated on does not drive this. Our original choice, a
grid masked to the convex hull of the trajectory's $(V,s)$ cloud, is open to a
fair objection: a spiking limit cycle is a closed loop, the interior of a loop is
never visited, and network values there are extrapolation presented as
interpolation. We therefore refit the same conductance form on six domains that
overlap rather than nest: the hull, three $\epsilon$-tubes of increasing radius
around the trajectory in the network's own input coordinates, the full
trajectory, and the strictly supervised trajectory $t\le\Ttrain$\,ms. The tubes
nest within one another but not within the hull, and the two trajectory domains
are the probe points themselves rather than grid subsets, so this is a family of
genuinely different views of the closure rather than a sequence of
restrictions. The estimate is stable across all of them, $\hat a$ ranging only
over $1.36$ to $1.65$ with standard % numok: six-domain refit range, results/identifiability/symbolic_domain_comparison.csv
deviations of $0.50$ to $0.90$. % numok: six-domain refit spread, results/identifiability/symbolic_domain_comparison.csv
We quote the supervised trajectory for every conductance estimate in the text,
since it is the one domain no reader can object to; the two symbolic figure
panels show the original hull fit, which agrees with it within seed noise and is
labelled as such.

That exercise also corrects something we had believed. We had attributed the need
for an off-trajectory domain to near-collinearity of the limit cycle, but that
diagnosis does not survive measurement for the fit it was invoked to justify. For
the two-term conductance form the
trajectory is perfectly well conditioned (condition number
$\valSymbolicCondTrajTrain$ on the trajectory against $\valSymbolicCondHull$ on
the hull --- the trajectory is the worse conditioned of the two, but both are far
from ill-conditioned), and it recovers marginally \emph{better} there. We did not measure the conditioning of
the six-term polynomial library on either domain, so we assert nothing about it.
The hull was never needed for the constrained fit.

% claim: regression-machinery-is-sound
The gap is the network's, not the regression's. Applying the identical estimator,
on the identical hull-restricted domain, to the \emph{analytic} calcium current
returns $a\approx\gcaMain$ and $b\approx\bTrue$ exactly, the true values. % numok: sanity fit console output, objective3_symbolic.jl:208-209
That is exact by construction and not by luck: the analytic current lies exactly
in the span of the estimator's two columns, so least squares returns the true
pair to machine precision on the hull domain, on the supervised trajectory, or on
any other subset that leaves the design matrix full rank. The control is
therefore a wiring check---it establishes that the estimator, the hull-restricted
domain and the mask are the ones we describe---rather than a test the fit could
have failed for a statistical reason. What it rules out is a broken or misapplied
regression, and that is all the inference needs: the spread in $\hat a$ is a
property of the trained closure, not of the fit.

% claim: sindy-does-not-sparsify
The unconstrained discovery attempt tells the same story from the opposite
direction. Sparse regression over the six-term library retains all five
thresholded terms at every seed ($\valSindyNTermsRetained$ of six; the intercept
is retained by construction and is never a candidate for elimination), never
producing a sparse equation, yet
the resulting fit still predicts the true current well
($R^2 = \valSymbolicRTwoSindyTrueMeanStd$). At the representative seed the
physical $Vs^2$ term is even fitted with the \emph{wrong sign} while that same fit
scores a high $R^2$ against the true current. This is the cleanest single
illustration that predictive accuracy and identifiability are different properties. The
network's small departures from the exact form leak into the correlated low-order
terms; this is an identifiability limit, not a defect of the sparse solver.

\subsection{What the data alone determine: a parametric reference}
\label{sec:results-parametric}

Everything so far says the conductance read off the closure is unreliable.
Nothing so far says \emph{why}, and the natural explanation is that one noisy
trajectory simply does not contain the information. That explanation is testable,
and it is wrong.

% claim: parametric-fit-identifies-conductance
Fitting $g_{Ca}$ and $E_{Ca}$ directly to the same trajectory, with the true
structure known and no network, recovers the conductance at every setting we
tested (Table~\ref{tab:parametric}). At the headline condition
$\hat g_{Ca} = \valParamFitGcaHatTwoMeanStd$ against a true $\gcaMain$, a
relative spread of about six per cent, with per-seed estimates confined to
\valParamFitGcaHatRangeTwo. The fit is statistically clean:
$\chi^2$ per data point, the statistic of \eqref{eq:chisq} divided by its
$\Nchisq$ residuals, averages $\valParamFitChiSqPerPointTwo$ over the seeds, so
the residuals sit at the known noise floor, which is what licenses the interval
below.

Three routes lead to the same precision, which is why we believe it.
The observed seed-to-seed spread is $\valParamFitGcaHatTwoStd$. The
profile likelihood, computed on the representative seed, puts its 95\% interval % numok: 95% confidence level, a convention
at $[1.6065, 2.1477]$. % numok: Delta-chi2 = 3.8415 crossings, linearly interpolated between the bracketing grid points of results/identifiability/profile_gca_gca2.0.csv; the Wald interval from conditioning.csv seed 1111 independently gives [1.6049, 2.1492]
The profile is evaluated on a grid of step $\ProfileStep$ in $g_{Ca}$, so we
quote the interpolated threshold crossings rather than the outermost grid points
that happen to fall under the threshold; the profile is convex on both flanks
there, so the interpolated interval is if anything marginally too narrow.
That interval belongs to one realisation and is centred on that realisation's
estimate, which is the lowest of the five, so it sits below the pooled mean and
two of the other seeds fall above its upper edge; % numok: per-seed estimates, results/identifiability/parametric_fit.csv
each realisation owns its own interval and the two quantities should not be read
as one. The Fisher information gives an asymptotic relative standard error of
$\valCondRelSeGcaTwoMeanStd$. Three routes to the same number, from the data, the
likelihood geometry and the sensitivity structure respectively
(Fig.~\ref{fig:parametric}a,b). The last two are not independent of one another:
the Fisher information here is the Gauss--Newton approximation to the very
$\chi^2$ surface the profile traces, built from first-order sensitivities at the
fitted optimum, so its agreement with the profile is evidence that the surface is
locally quadratic near that optimum rather than a second, separate measurement of
the width. Only the seed-to-seed spread is independent of the other two, and it
agrees with both. Four of the five conductances distilled from the closure lie
well outside that interval; the fifth sits on its lower edge, inside by a margin
far below the resolution of the profile itself. % numok: 4 of 5 outside the interpolated interval [1.6065, 2.1477], traj-train rows of results/identifiability/symbolic_domain_comparison.csv; seed 3333 at 1.6069 is inside by 0.0004

% claim: closure-induced-nonidentifiability
Set that beside the closure. On identical data, the conductance estimated
parametrically is $\valParamFitGcaHatTwoMeanStd$ and the conductance distilled
from the neural closure is $\valSymbolicAHatTrajTrainMeanStd$: the relative
spread grows from roughly six per cent to roughly forty per cent, and
the per-seed span widens from \valParamFitGcaHatRangeTwo{} to
\valSymbolicAHatRangeTrajTrain. On the wider hull domain the closure is worse
still ($\valSymbolicAHatMeanStd$, spanning \valSymbolicAHatRange), so the choice
of domain does not flatter the comparison. The contrast is
starker at the physiological conductance, where the parametric fit still returns
$\valParamFitGcaHatPointFourMeanStd$ against a true $\gcaPhys$ while the symbolic
route returns the wrong sign entirely on the hull domain
($\hat a = \valSymbolicAHatNegControl$, single seed) and an estimate
indistinguishable from zero on the supervised trajectory
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results-retrain}); neither is usable.

The data were the same in every one of these comparisons, but the representation
was not the only thing that differed, and it is worth measuring how much of the
gap it actually owns. The parametric fit minimises the noise-weighted $\chi^2$ of
\eqref{eq:chisq} by multi-start Nelder--Mead; the closure descends the unweighted
\eqref{eq:loss}, which is \LossShareV\,\% voltage, by Adam and BFGS. So we repeat
the parametric fit under \eqref{eq:loss} --- the closure's own objective, on the
same data, with the same true functional form, the same two free parameters and
the same starting points, changing nothing but which residual is minimised. It
returns $\valParamFitGcaHatTwoSseFullMeanStd$: a relative spread of roughly
twenty per cent, against six per cent under the likelihood. Descending a
voltage-dominated objective, rather than representing the current with a network,
therefore accounts for about a threefold inflation on its own. The closure's
forty per cent sits a further twofold above \emph{that}. % claim: closure-induced-nonidentifiability
The representation is not innocent, but it is responsible for roughly half of the
gap on a log scale, not all of it, and we quote the twofold figure rather than
the sixfold one wherever the two arms are compared. (The voltage-only objective
returns the same estimate to three decimals, which is what
\eqref{eq:loss} being \LossShareV\,\% voltage predicts.)

We are careful about the inference this licenses, because the comparison is not
between equals and we do not want it read as one. A two-parameter model given the
true functional form should beat a $\NparamUde$-parameter closure given no form
at all, and it does, on forecast error too: the parametric fit reaches
$\valParamFitForecastVRmseTwoMeanStd$\,mV against the UDE's
$\valUdeForecastVRmseMeanStd$\,mV. The claim is therefore not that two
equally good fits disagree. It is one-directional and it does not need to be
more: because the data pin the conductance to about six per cent, the closure's
forty per cent cannot be blamed on the data.

Two further readings of Table~\ref{tab:parametric} are worth stating. The
parametric fit degrades \emph{gracefully} as the calcium signal shrinks, its
relative spread widening from $2.9$ to $14.9$ per cent across the sweep % numok: relative spreads, results/identifiability/parametric_fit.csv
while never losing the sign or the order of magnitude, where the closure fails
discontinuously. And the two calcium parameters are not equally well determined:
$\hat E_{Ca} = \valParamFitEcaHatTwoMeanStd$\,mV carries a wider relative
interval than the conductance, the log-parameter information matrix is
ill-conditioned (condition number about $\valCondNumberTwo$, a five-seed mean
whose printed precision is not significant), and the two parameters are almost perfectly
anti-correlated. There \emph{is} a sloppy direction here --- the anti-correlated
trade-off between conductance and reversal potential --- and, orthogonal to it, a
combination of the two that the data constrain far better than either parameter
alone. That pairing of one flat and one stiff direction is the classic signature
of a sloppy, rather than structurally non-identifiable, pair \citep{raue2009, wieland2021}. At the
physiological $g_{Ca}=\gcaPhys$ the reversal potential's 95\% profile interval % numok: 95% confidence level, a convention
runs from $60$ to $300$\,mV: bounded below, but still open at the top of the % numok: profile CI bounds over an 81-point grid on [0,300] mV, results/identifiability/profile_eca_gca0.4.csv
range we scanned, so $E_{Ca}$ alone is practically non-identifiable there. Sloppy is not the same as
non-identifiable, and the distinction is the whole point: both parameters retain
finite, small standard errors at the headline condition, and the conductance
retains a usable one even at the physiological value.

\subsection{Is the residual gap trainable? An aggressive-retrain control}
\label{sec:results-retrain}

% claim: retrain-does-not-close-gap
% claim: retrain-improves-loss-worsens-recovery
The remaining objection is that the coefficient gap is under-training. It is not,
and the control that shows so is sharper than we first realised
(Table~\ref{tab:retrain}). We quadrupled the optimisation budget, to \AdamRe{}
Adam and \BfgsRe{} BFGS iterations with everything else identical, and re-ran
the same symbolic pipeline.

The training objective genuinely improved. Comparable losses were not recorded at
the time, but the trained weights were saved, so the before-loss can be recovered
without retraining anything: rebuild the exact objective and evaluate it at the
saved parameters. Doing so gives $\valTrainLossBeforeMeanStd$ before against
$\valTrainLossAfterMeanStd$ after, a reduction of about \LossDropPct\,per cent.
(The recovery is trustworthy because the same procedure reproduces the
\emph{recorded} after-loss to a relative difference of zero.) BFGS also reported
success on all five seeds ($\valRetrainBfgsOk$).

Both of those numbers deserve a reference point, and \eqref{eq:loss} supplies
one. Evaluated at the maximum-likelihood $(g_{Ca}, E_{Ca})$ of
Sec.~\ref{sec:methods-parametric} --- the true structure, correctly fitted, on
the same six channels and the same $\Ntrain$ samples --- the identical objective
scores $\valParamFitSseFullTwoMeanStd$. Both UDE losses are \emph{below} it, and
the retrained one is well below. A model cannot score better than the correct
physics on the data the physics generated unless it is fitting the noise
realisation, so the extra descent is not motion along a harmless flat direction;
it is over-fitting, and the recovery quantities below are what it costs. (That
reference is the objective at the $\chi^2$ optimum rather than at its own
minimum, so it slightly overstates the floor: on the one seed for which we
computed the full surface, the objective-optimal pair scores under half a per
cent lower, against a margin here some twenty times larger.)

And yet every recovery quantity got \emph{worse and noisier}. Both sides of this
comparison are quoted on the convex-hull domain, because that is where the
retrained closures were distilled, so it is like-for-like; the before-value
differs from the supervised-trajectory figure quoted earlier for that reason
alone. The conductance moved farther from the truth and its standard deviation
inflated
($\hat a$: $\valSymbolicAHatMeanStd \to \valRetrainAHatMeanStd$), agreement with
the true current fell
($R^2$: $\valSymbolicRTwoCondTrueMeanStd \to \valRetrainRTwoCondTrueMeanStd$), and
even the trajectory forecast degraded
($\valUdeForecastVRmseMeanStd \to \valRetrainForecastVRmseMeanStd$\,mV).

% claim: retrain-negative-control-replicates
The same holds at the physiological conductance, which is worth checking
separately because that is where the method fails hardest. Retraining the
$g_{Ca}=\gcaPhys$ case at the same aggressive budget leaves the conductance
wrong-signed and moves it further from the truth \emph{on the convex-hull
domain}, the domain these retrained closures were distilled on
($\valGcaFourRetrainAHatBefore \to \valGcaFourRetrainAHatAfter$ against a true
$+\gcaPhys$), and agreement with the true current falls below zero there
($R^2$: $\valGcaFourRetrainRTwoBefore \to \valGcaFourRetrainRTwoAfter$), meaning
the distilled form becomes a worse predictor of the calcium current than that
current's own mean. On the supervised trajectory both budgets instead return an
estimate indistinguishable from zero. The two domains disagree by roughly a
factor of six at this conductance, which is why each number here is labelled with
the domain it came from; but they fail in the same direction, and the failure is
the closure's rather than the data's, since the direct fit still recovers
$\valParamFitGcaHatPointFourMeanStd$ here
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results-parametric}).

That combination is the result, and it is sharper than a harmlessly flat
direction. It is not that more optimisation achieved nothing, which would only
show the optimiser had already converged. It is that more optimisation
demonstrably \emph{succeeded} at what it was asked to do, fit the \emph{noisy}
training data better, and everything we wanted to read out of the model got worse
as it did. The degradation reaches inside the training window itself: on the very
samples the objective scores, distance from the \emph{true} trajectory rises from
$\valUdeTrainVRmseMeanStd$\,mV to $\valRetrainTrainVRmseMeanStd$\,mV, at the same
time as the forecast triples. The extra descent buys agreement with one noise
realisation at the cost of agreement with the truth, and it degrades the closure
and the trajectory together rather than sliding freely along a direction the data
leave open.

We should say plainly what our protocol does not do. Neither the baseline nor the
retrain holds out a validation split, and neither applies an early-stopping
criterion: both run a fixed Adam-then-BFGS schedule to its iteration budget and
report the final iterate, so nothing in the pipeline could have detected or
halted that descent. The control is therefore evidence about the
\emph{optimiser}, not about generalisation. It establishes that the coefficient
gap is not a shortage of descent, and that further descent is actively harmful;
it cannot separate how much of that harm is over-fitting the noise realisation
from how much is motion along a direction of the closure's parameter space that
the voltage does not constrain. Both readings carry the same practical
conclusion---training longer is not the repair---and neither licenses treating
the extra iterations as innocuous.

% claim: retrain-seed-decoupling
The representative seed makes the mechanism concrete, and we flag at once that on
this one axis it is the most favourable of the five: its retrained forecast error
is the lowest of the five, and over all five seeds the retrain roughly tripled the
mean, as reported above. Under the aggressive
retrain its forecast voltage accuracy is essentially unchanged
($\valBaselineRepSeedForecastVRmse \to \valRetrainRepSeedForecastVRmse$\,mV, well
within the five-seed baseline spread), while its agreement with the true calcium
current collapses
($R^2$: $\valBaselineRepSeedRTwoCondTrue \to \valRetrainRepSeedRTwoCondTrue$). The
same trajectory quality is achieved by a different, and no better, closure:
trajectory quality and closure identity moved independently, which is exactly
what a flat direction in the loss means, observed directly rather than inferred.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

% claim: ude-recovers-trajectory-not-coefficients
% claim: closure-induced-nonidentifiability
The picture assembled across these experiments is a single, scoped finding: the
UDE recovers the trajectory but not the hidden current's coefficients, and the
residual gap is a property of the \emph{representation}, not of the data.
Voltage is forecast to sub-millivolt accuracy over the whole un-trained
remainder; the closure agrees closely with the true current's shape; and the
conductance read off it is only order-correct, with neither a signal-amplitude
sweep nor a fourfold optimisation budget closing the gap.

What turns this from a failure report into a characterisation is a chain of
controls that removes the alternative explanations one at a time. The same
estimator recovers the true coefficients when fitted to the analytic current, so
the fault is not the regression. The result is unchanged across six fitting
domains including the strictly supervised trajectory, so it is not an artifact of
where we evaluated the closure. The gap widens toward low conductance in a
controlled sweep, so it is not an accident of the headline setting. It survives,
indeed grows, under quadrupled optimisation that measurably improved the training
loss while worsening agreement with the truth even on the trained samples, so it
is not under-training and the extra descent was not innocuous. And the residual explanation, that one noisy
trajectory cannot determine the conductance, is ruled out by direct measurement:
the same trajectory determines it to about six per cent. What is left is the
closure itself.

That conclusion has a name in the identifiability literature. Following
\citet{loman2026structural}, a parameter can be structurally identifiable while
an unknown \emph{function} standing in for the same physics is not, and
\citet{loman2025ude} separate exactly these two notions for universal
differential equations. Our experiments are an empirical instance: parametric
identifiability holds and functional identifiability fails, on identical data,
with the representation the axis we set out to vary. It is not the only thing
that changes between the two arms, as Sec.~\ref{sec:methods-parametric} records,
so the size of the gap should not be read as a pure representation effect; what
the comparison establishes is the direction, that an estimator does recover the
conductance from this trajectory and the closure does not.
A flexible closure has directions of
variation that the two-parameter model does not, and the observed voltage does not
constrain them.

% claim: predictive-accuracy-is-not-identifiability
The transferable lesson is that predictive accuracy on the observed channel is
not evidence that a learned physical term has been identified. Three independent
instances make the point here: sparse regression attains a high $R^2$ against the
true current while fitting its dominant term with the wrong sign; five seeds reach
indistinguishable forecast accuracy with conductances spanning more than a factor
of two on the supervised trajectory, and more than four on the hull; and the retrain holds forecast accuracy on the representative seed while
its agreement with the true current collapses. Parity plots and rollout error,
the two diagnostics UDE studies most often report, are exactly the diagnostics
that cannot see this failure. Our result is continuous with the
identifiable-combinations theory of \citet{walch2016}: the network pins the
product that determines the current's contribution to $dV/dt$, not the
factorisation into a conductance and a gating shape, and a flexible closure
absorbs that ambiguity rather than resolving it.

% claim: identifiability-is-the-general-lesson
We state the general form of this as a recommendation rather than a theorem,
because our evidence is one system. Where a UDE's learned term is meant to be
interpreted rather than merely simulated, its identifiability should be
established before its coefficients are read as physical quantities, and the
question to ask is not only whether the data determine the parameter but whether
they still determine it once the term is represented by a flexible closure. Those
are different questions, and here they had different answers.

Two diagnostics follow, both cheap. The first is a seed-spread check on the
recovered coefficients, which would have caught the failure in this study
immediately and costs only a re-run. The second applies whenever the functional
form is even approximately known: fit it parametrically and compare. That
reference point converts a bare spread into an attribution, because it separates
what the data cannot supply from what the closure discards. The mechanism we
observe, a flat direction along which the closure varies while the observable does
not, is general; its presence in any particular system is a hypothesis to be
tested, not assumed. The choice of $g_{Ca}=\gcaMain$ was made in that spirit: not
to make the method succeed, but to place the system in the regime where recovery
is possible enough that its residual failure is informative.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

% claim: closure-induced-nonidentifiability
The binding limitation is that we have varied the representation on exactly one
axis. We compare a two-parameter model against one $\NparamUde$-parameter
closure of one architecture, so we can say that flexibility cost identifiability
here, but not how much flexibility is too much, nor where between those two
extremes identifiability is lost. The obvious next experiment is a ladder of
intermediate closures, from a single learned scalar through low-order bases to
the full network, measuring where the conductance stops being recoverable. We do
not run it and make no prediction about its outcome. A second axis we did not
vary is the closure's input set, which fixes what degeneracies are even
expressible.

The comparison at the heart of the paper is also not a comparison of equals, and
we would rather state that than let a reader discover it. The parametric fit
knows the true functional form and estimates two numbers; the closure knows
nothing and estimates $\NparamUde$. It also forecasts better. The inference we
draw is correspondingly one-directional: because the data pin the conductance,
the closure's failure to pin it is not the data's fault. We do not claim the two
methods are otherwise comparable, and a reader who wants the converse claim,
that a closure could match parametric precision given the right constraints, will
not find it supported here. The two spreads we set side by side are not even the
same kind of quantity. The parametric $\hat g_{Ca}$ is a parameter the objective
estimates directly, and its scatter is the scatter of an estimator with a
likelihood behind it; $\hat a$ is an ordinary-least-squares projection of an
already-learned function onto a two-term basis, so its scatter compounds what the
closure learned with what that projection discards. We therefore read the gap
between them as an order-of-magnitude contrast, not as one error bar against
another.

Three further limitations bound the claims. The training-window and noise
ablations remain single-seed and carry no error bars; only the conductance sweep
has been repeated
across seeds, and we claim from it only the end-to-end trend, since two of its
three adjacent steps fall within seed noise. No statistical test is run anywhere,
and $n=\valUdeForecastVRmseN$ would support almost none; every comparative
statement is an equivalence-within-noise statement, not an ordering. And every
conclusion still rests on one trajectory: one applied drive, one initial
condition, one \Tfull\,ms window. That is no longer the explanation of our
negative result, but it does bound its reach, and whether multiple trajectories
would restore identifiability \emph{through the closure} is untested, as is
which additional measurement would tighten the $g_{Ca}$--$E_{Ca}$ trade-off we
observe directly in Sec.~\ref{sec:results-parametric}. We make no prediction
about either.

% claim: seeds-confound-init-and-noise
Finally, the seeds redraw the network initialisation and the noise realisation
jointly, so every spread we report is a joint init-and-data spread and the two
cannot be separated from these runs.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Embedding known physics and learning one hidden ionic current makes a \Rcap\,ms
extrapolation both accurate and dependable, even when only voltage is observed,
and yields a closure that agrees closely with the true calcium current's shape.
It does not yield the current's conductance. The reason is not the one that would
be assumed: the single noisy trajectory determines that conductance to about six
per cent, as a direct parametric fit on the very same data shows. It is the
flexible closure that loses it, and a chain of controls leaves nothing else
standing, since the regression is exonerated, the fitting domain is immaterial,
the signal-amplitude sweep moves about eightfold end to end, and quadrupled
optimisation lowers the training loss while worsening every recovered quantity.

The practical consequence for scientific machine learning is a caution with a
concrete remedy. A UDE coefficient is a physical estimate only when the data
identify it \emph{through the representation actually used}, which is a stricter
condition than the data identifying it at all. An inexpensive seed-spread check
flags when that condition fails, and where the functional form is even
approximately known, a parametric fit on the same data says whether to blame the
data or the closure. Predictive accuracy on the observed channel answers neither
question, which is precisely why it is the wrong diagnostic to have been relying
on.

\section{Model equations and gating kinetics}
\label{app:eqns}

The classical gating kinetics are (constants verbatim from the cited source; each
line names its provenance):
\begin{align*}
  \alpha_m(V) &= \frac{0.1\,(V+40)}{1-e^{-(V+40)/10}}, &  % numok: HH kinetics, src/hh_core.jl:50-63
  \beta_m(V)  &= 4\,e^{-(V+65)/18}, \\                    % numok: HH kinetics, src/hh_core.jl:50-63
  \alpha_h(V) &= 0.07\,e^{-(V+65)/20}, &                  % numok: HH kinetics, src/hh_core.jl:50-63
  \beta_h(V)  &= \frac{1}{1+e^{-(V+35)/10}}, \\           % numok: HH kinetics, src/hh_core.jl:50-63
  \alpha_n(V) &= \frac{0.01\,(V+55)}{1-e^{-(V+55)/10}}, & % numok: HH kinetics, src/hh_core.jl:50-63
  \beta_n(V)  &= 0.125\,e^{-(V+65)/80},                   % numok: HH kinetics, src/hh_core.jl:50-63
\end{align*}
with the removable singularities of $\alpha_m,\alpha_n$ evaluated at their
analytic limits. The two added gates use steady-state activations $p_\infty(V) = [1+e^{-(V+50)/6}]^{-1}$ and $s_\infty(V) = [1+e^{-(V+25)/9}]^{-1}$ % numok: p_inf/s_inf, src/hh_core.jl:65,70
with first-order relaxation at fixed time constants $\tau_p,\tau_s$
(Table~\ref{tab:params}). Both are logistic approximations, not kinetics transcribed from a source: the high-threshold calcium activation of the source model \citep{reuveni1993} is specified by voltage-dependent rate functions whose steady state sits lower and rises more steeply than the single logistic used here. We approximate it because nothing reported in this paper depends on the shape of $s_\infty$ --- the gate kinetics are known physics, supplied identically to the data generator, to the closure, and to the parametric fit, so they cancel from every comparison we draw, and the recovery target is the pair $(g_{Ca},E_{Ca})$, not the activation curve. Of the four added-gate activation constants only one is literature-anchored: the persistent-sodium half-point follows
\citet{magistretti1999}, whose reported activation slopes are nonetheless all steeper than the slope factor we pair with it. The other three --- the persistent-sodium slope and both calcium activation constants --- are choices, and Table~\ref{tab:params} labels them as such. The calcium reversal potential is a separate matter and does follow
\citet{reuveni1993}.
The persistent-sodium \emph{conductance} $g_{NaP}=\mgNaP$ is likewise labelled a
choice rather than a citation, and deliberately so: it is of the same order as
the persistent-sodium conductance carried by the models of \citet{golomb1997}
once that conductance is rescaled to our sodium conductance, but we did not
verify a printed value against the original, and we prefer to mark it as ours
rather than attribute a number we have not checked. Nothing in this paper turns
on it. It is a constant of the ground-truth generator, supplied identically to
the data, to the closure and to the parametric fit, so it cancels from every
comparison we draw; the recovery target is $(g_{Ca}, E_{Ca})$.

\section{Full hyperparameters and training details}
\label{app:hyper}

The UDE calcium closure is a multilayer perceptron
$\ArchUde$ with $\tanh$ activations and inputs $\NNinputs$
($\NparamUde$ parameters); the black-box neural ODE is $\ArchNode$ with layer
normalisation and inputs of the six normalised states and raw time
($\approx\NparamNode$ parameters).
Both are integrated with an explicit fifth-order Runge--Kutta method at absolute
and relative tolerances of $\Tol$, under an interpolating continuous adjoint with
reverse-mode vector--Jacobian products. Training is two-stage: Adam at learning
rate $\Lr$ followed by a BFGS polish (initial step-norm $\Lr$) wrapped so that
a failed polish degrades to the Adam result. Budgets are Adam \AdamUde/BFGS
\BfgsUde{} for the UDE and Adam \AdamNode/BFGS \BfgsNode{} for the neural ODE
as originally configured. The two repaired neural-ODE controls of
Sec.~\ref{sec:results-forecasting} (rows 2 and 3 of Table~\ref{tab:forecast}) use
the UDE's budget instead, Adam \AdamUde/BFGS \BfgsUde, and the autonomous one
drops the time input so its first layer is $\ArchNodeAuto$. The
aggressive-retrain control of Sec.~\ref{sec:results-retrain} uses Adam
\AdamRe/BFGS \BfgsRe. The five seeds are $\{1111,2222,3333,4444,5555\}$; % numok: seed set, experiments_runner.jl:57
the representative seed for single-run panels is the first of these, verified
median-typical by the guardrail of Sec.~\ref{sec:methods-train}.

Two metric definitions carry knobs the reported numbers depend on. The rollout
horizon is the elapsed time from the first forecast sample until
$|V_{\mathrm{pred}}-V_{\mathrm{true}}|$ exceeds \Ttube\,mV and \emph{stays} above
it for at least \Rsus\,ms; at the \dtSample\,ms sampling interval that is five
consecutive samples, so an excursion past the tube lasting four samples or fewer
is discarded, a non-finite error counts as a breach, and a trajectory that never
breaches returns the \Rcap\,ms cap. Because the grid is \Ndata{} uniform samples
over the \Tfull\,ms window, the first forecast sample lies at \Tfcast\,ms rather
than at \Ttrain\,ms exactly, and horizons are measured from there. Spikes are
local maxima of $V$ above \Vthresh\,mV, with peaks closer together than
\SpikeSep\,ms merged as refractory and the taller kept; predicted peaks are then
matched greedily to true peaks and a pair counts only if it falls within
\SpikeTol\,ms, so a true peak with no partner inside that tolerance contributes
nothing to the mean absolute deviation. Statements about reproducing the spike
count are statements about these settings.

Several failure paths in the pipeline are silent by design, and the reported
tables inherit them. A full-span solve that stops early or throws is caught and
returned NaN-padded to the full sample grid with a warning only, so a run that
failed to integrate still produces metric rows rather than an error. The RMSE
helper then drops non-finite pairs before averaging, so a metric computed from a
partially padded prediction is an RMSE over whatever part of the window stayed
finite, and it carries no marker recording how much of the window that was; this
is why the diverged neural-ODE voltage errors are reported as spans across seeds
rather than as a mean and standard deviation. During training, a step whose solve
returns the wrong number of samples is scored with a large fixed penalty instead
of raising, which keeps the optimiser moving but makes such steps
indistinguishable in the loss history. Finally, the two-stage trainer records
whether the BFGS polish succeeded, but that flag is written out only for the
aggressive-retrain and neural-ODE parity runs; for the baseline and ablation
sweep it is discarded, so a silent degradation to the Adam result would not be
visible in those tables. We report this rather than repair it, because changing
any of it would change the numbers.
