\begin{abstract}
Universal differential equations embed a neural network inside a mechanistic
model to learn a term the modeller does not know. In conductance-based neuron
models the natural application is to learn an unknown ionic current from an
observed voltage trace and then read the recovered current's coefficients as
biophysical estimates. We test whether that reading is earned. Replacing the
calcium current in a six-state Hodgkin--Huxley model with a neural closure
$U(V,s)$ and training on the first \Ttrain\,ms of a single \Tfull\,ms noisy
trajectory, the resulting model forecasts held-out voltage to
$\valUdeForecastVRmseMeanStd$\,mV over $\valUdeForecastVRmseN$ seeds, yet the
conductance distilled from it is only order-correct:
$\hat{a} = \valSymbolicAHatTrajTrainMeanStd$ against a true $\gcaMain$.
The usual explanation --- that one trajectory does not carry the information ---
is wrong, and we show it is wrong on the same data. A direct two-parameter fit
of $(g_{Ca}, E_{Ca})$, with no network, returns
$g_{Ca} = \valParamFitGcaHatTwoMeanStd$ at a relative standard error of
$\valCondRelSeGcaTwoMean$. The information is present; the flexible closure
loses it. Repeating that fit under the closure's own voltage-dominated
objective, changing nothing else, isolates the objective as the larger single
cause and bounds the representation's own contribution at about a factor of two.
What fails is not parametric but \emph{functional} identifiability --- a
distinction with direct consequences for anyone reading coefficients off a
learned closure in an imperfect simulator.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A universal differential equation (UDE) keeps the parts of a mechanistic model
that are trusted and replaces the parts that are not with a neural network
\citep{rackauckas2020universal}. The appeal for simulator-based science is
obvious: the known physics constrains the fit, the network absorbs the
discrepancy, and a post-hoc symbolic step can in principle read the learned term
back out as an interpretable expression. In conductance-based neuroscience that
promise has a precise form. Hide one ionic current, learn it from a voltage
trace, distil the learned function into a conductance expression, and read its
leading coefficient as a conductance density.

We test the last step, which is the one that carries the scientific weight and
the one that is least often checked. Our system is a six-state Hodgkin--Huxley
neuron \citep{hodgkin1952} extended with a persistent sodium current
and a high-threshold calcium current; the calcium current is hidden and replaced
by a neural closure. The forecasting result is unambiguous and good. The
recovery result is not, and the reason turns out to be the opposite of the one
usually offered.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\emph{(i)} A UDE that keeps the known Hodgkin--Huxley kinetics and learns only
the hidden calcium current forecasts held-out voltage to
$\valUdeForecastVRmseMeanStd$\,mV and never leaves a \Ttube\,mV error tube on any
seed, against a black-box neural ODE baseline we first had to repair: matching
its optimisation budget changes nothing, while removing a raw time input from the
right-hand side of an autonomous system improves it by more than an order of
magnitude, so the failure is architectural rather than a shortage of
optimisation. \emph{(ii)} The conductance distilled from that closure is only
order-correct, spanning \valSymbolicAHatRangeTrajTrain{} across seeds on the
supervised trajectory and bracketing the truth on both sides. \emph{(iii)} A
direct parametric fit to the \emph{same} trajectory recovers the conductance to
about six per cent, so the data determine what the closure does not.
\emph{(iv)} A matched-objective control separates the causes: the objective
accounts for the larger share, and the remainder bounds the representation's own
contribution at about a factor of two.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Model and data.}
The ground-truth generator is a Hodgkin--Huxley membrane equation with sodium,
potassium and leak currents, a persistent sodium current, and a calcium current
$I_{Ca} = g_{Ca}s^2(V - E_{Ca})$ driven by a single activation gate $s$. Five
gates ($m$, $h$, $n$, $p$, $s$) and the voltage give six states. We integrate one
trajectory over $[0,\Tfull]$\,ms from rest at $V_0 = \Vrest$\,mV, sample \Ndata{}
uniform points, and corrupt every channel with Ornstein--Uhlenbeck noise at
\NoisePct\,\% of that channel's own standard deviation. Training uses the
$\Ntrain$ samples with $t \le \Ttrain$\,ms; everything reported as a forecast is
the tail of the same uninterrupted integration, scored against the \emph{clean}
trajectory. We run $\valUdeForecastVRmseN$ seeds, each redrawing both the noise
realisation and the network initialisation.

The headline conductance is $g_{Ca} = \gcaMain$, five times the physiological
density; the physiological value $\gcaPhys$ is retained as a negative control.
That choice is a limitation we return to in Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}, and it is
derived rather than tuned: rescaling the calcium density of a published
intrinsically-bursting pyramidal cell \citep{pospischil2008, reuveni1993}
to this model's squid-axon sodium conductance gives $\gcaPhys$.

\paragraph{Closure and objective.}
The calcium term is replaced by a multilayer perceptron
$U(\NNinputs)$ with $\NparamUde$ parameters, trained by Adam
(\AdamUde{} iterations) followed by a BFGS polish (\BfgsUde{}) under a continuous
adjoint. The loss is a plain sum of squares over the sampled states, in the
states' native units:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{full}} = \sum_{i=1}^{\Ntrain}\ \sum_{c=1}^{6}
  \bigl(x_c(t_i) - \hat{x}_c(t_i)\bigr)^2 .
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
There is no normalisation and there are no channel weights. This matters more
than it looks: each channel's residual scales with its own noise magnitude, and
the voltage trace's standard deviation exceeds the mean gate standard deviation
by more than $\GateSdRatio\times$, so at the noise floor the voltage term carries
\LossShareV\,\% of the expected value of \eqref{eq:loss}. The objective is
already almost a voltage-only objective, and we return to the consequence in
Sec.~\ref{sec:decomp}.

\paragraph{Distillation.}
The trained closure is distilled by sparse regression, \emph{constrained to} the
biophysical conductance form $s^2(aV+b)$, on the supervised trajectory. The
form is \emph{supplied} to the estimator, not found by it: what follows is
agreement with an assumed form, not discovery of one. Unconstrained symbolic
regression over a six-term library failed to sparsify, which is itself an
identifiability observation and is reported in the supplementary material rather
than hidden.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Forecasting, and a baseline worth comparing against}
\label{sec:forecast}

The UDE never breaches the \Ttube\,mV tube on any seed. Its rollout horizon is
therefore right-censored at the metric's ceiling ($\valUdeForecastRollout$\,ms;
a value equal to that bound means the tube was never left, so the true horizon is
unknown), and its forecast voltage RMSE is $\valUdeForecastVRmseMeanStd$\,mV.
Restricting the loss to the voltage row alone gives
$\valUdeVoltageForecastVRmseMeanStd$\,mV, equivalent within seed noise --- as it
nearly must be, given that voltage already carries \LossShareV\,\% of the
unweighted objective at the noise floor. The five gate trajectories, which never
enter the loss in that condition, are still propagated accurately by the retained
kinetics.

A black-box neural ODE is only worth comparing against if it has been given a
fair chance, and ours had not. As initially configured it trained for fewer Adam
iterations than the UDE and took \emph{raw time} as an input alongside the six
states. The second is the serious one: the true system is autonomous, so a
right-hand side that reads the clock is strictly more expressive than the physics
requires. Raising the budget to the UDE's own \AdamUde/\BfgsUde{} while keeping
that architecture does not help --- the rollout horizon drifts down to
$\valNodeParityTimeRolloutMeanStd$\,ms and the forecast error does not fall,
spanning $\valNodeParityTimeVRmseMin$--$\valNodeParityTimeVRmseMax$\,mV across
seeds. We give that error as a span rather than a mean $\pm$ SD because both
time-indexed configurations blow up rather than degrade, and a dispersion
statistic over blown-up solves is not an error bar. Removing the time input at
that same budget changes the picture entirely: the horizon reaches
$\valNodeParityAutoRolloutMeanStd$\,ms and the median forecast error falls to
$\valNodeParityAutoVRmseMedian$\,mV, with four of the five seeds reproducing the
spike count exactly. So the catastrophic failure of the original baseline was an
artifact of an architecture nobody should have used on an autonomous system, and
we report the repaired one instead. The comparison that survives is a magnitude
one --- $\valUdeForecastVRmseMeanStd$\,mV against a median of
$\valNodeParityAutoVRmseMedian$\,mV --- not the categorical claim the broken
baseline would have licensed.

\subsection{The conductance read off the closure}

Constrained to the conductance form, the distilled closure reproduces the true
calcium current with $R^2 = \valSymbolicRTwoTrajTrainMeanStd$ on the supervised
trajectory, and describes the network's own output about as well
($R^2 = \valSymbolicRTwoNnTrajTrainMeanStd$). The shape is right.

The scale is not. The conductance implied by that same fit is
$\hat{a} = \valSymbolicAHatTrajTrainMeanStd$ against a true $\gcaMain$, spanning
\valSymbolicAHatRangeTrajTrain{} across the $\valUdeForecastVRmseN$ seeds ---
a spread that brackets the truth on both sides and is wide enough that a single
run would licence almost any conclusion. Curve-fit quality and coefficient
identifiability are different properties, and this is the gap between them.

\subsection{The data are not at fault}
\label{sec:parametric}

The natural reading of that scatter is that one short noisy trajectory does not
carry enough information about a hidden current to pin its conductance. We can
test that directly, and it does not survive.

We fit the two calcium parameters $(g_{Ca}, E_{Ca})$ to the \emph{same} single
noisy trajectory, with the true functional form known and no network anywhere in
the loop, minimising a noise-weighted $\chi^2$ by multi-start Nelder--Mead. The
fit returns $g_{Ca} = \valParamFitGcaHatTwoMeanStd$ against a true \gcaMain{}, an
error of about six per cent, at $\chi^2$ per point of
$\valParamFitChiSqPerPointTwo$ --- that is, sitting exactly at the known noise
floor. Three routes agree on the precision: an asymptotic relative standard error
of $\valCondRelSeGcaTwoMean$, the observed seed spread, and a profile-likelihood
interval. Only the seed spread is independent of the other two --- the asymptotic
error is the Gauss--Newton approximation to the same $\chi^2$ surface the profile
traces --- and it agrees with both. The representative seed's own estimate is the lowest
of the five, and the 95\% profile interval centred on \emph{that} estimate is % numok: 95% confidence level, a convention
$[1.6065, 2.1477]$; % numok: Delta-chi2 = 3.8415 crossings, linearly interpolated between the bracketing grid points of results/identifiability/profile_gca_gca2.0.csv; the Wald interval independently gives [1.6049, 2.1492]
each realisation owns its own interval. Four of the five conductances distilled
from the closure fall clearly outside that interval; the fifth sits on its lower
edge (Fig.~\ref{fig:ident}).

So the trajectory does determine the conductance, and the closure is what loses
it. We are careful about the inference this licenses. A two-parameter model
handed the true functional form \emph{should} beat a $\NparamUde$-parameter
closure handed no form, and it also forecasts about twice as well
($\valParamFitForecastVRmseTwoMeanStd$\,mV against
$\valUdeForecastVRmseMeanStd$\,mV). The claim is therefore not that two equally
good fits disagree. It is one-directional and it is sufficient: because some
estimator recovers the conductance to six per cent from this trajectory, the
closure's scatter cannot be blamed on the data.

\subsection{What owns the gap}
\label{sec:decomp}

The data were held fixed across that comparison, but the representation was not
the only thing that changed. The parametric arm minimises the noise-weighted
$\chi^2$ by multi-start Nelder--Mead; the closure descends the unweighted
\eqref{eq:loss}, which is \LossShareV\,\% voltage, by Adam and BFGS. So we repeat
the parametric fit under \eqref{eq:loss} --- the closure's own objective, same
data, same true functional form, same two free parameters, same starting points,
changing nothing but which residual is minimised. It returns
$\valParamFitGcaHatTwoSseFullMeanStd$: a relative spread of roughly twenty per
cent, against six under the likelihood.

The objective therefore accounts for about a threefold inflation on its own, and
the closure's roughly forty per cent sits a further twofold above \emph{that}.
The representation is not innocent, but it owns \emph{at most} about two-fifths
of the gap on a log scale --- less than the objective's share, and an upper bound
rather than a value, because this control holds the optimiser fixed and cannot
separate it from the representation. We quote that twofold figure, and never the
sixfold one, wherever the two arms are compared.

\subsection{Nor is it under-training}

The remaining objection is that the closure simply had not converged. Quadrupling
the budget to \AdamRe/\BfgsRe{} lowers the training objective from
$\valTrainLossBefore$ to $\valTrainLossAfter$, a reduction of about
\LossDropPct\,per cent --- and makes every recovery quantity worse. Both sides of
that comparison are quoted \emph{on the convex-hull domain}, because that is
where the retrained closures were distilled, so it is like-for-like; the
before-values therefore differ from the supervised-trajectory figures quoted
above for that reason alone. On the hull the conductance moves farther from the
truth and its spread inflates
($\hat a$: $\valSymbolicAHatMeanStd \to \valRetrainAHatMeanStd$), agreement with
the true current falls
($R^2$: $\valSymbolicRTwoCondTrueMeanStd \to \valRetrainRTwoCondTrueMeanStd$),
and even the trajectory forecast degrades
($\valUdeForecastVRmseMeanStd \to \valRetrainForecastVRmseMeanStd$\,mV). The
optimiser succeeded and the science got worse, which is the point: more descent
on this objective buys a better fit to the noise realisation, not a better
closure. A reference point makes that concrete --- evaluated at the
maximum-likelihood $(g_{Ca}, E_{Ca})$ of the true structure, the identical
objective scores $\valParamFitSseFullTwo$, so both losses above sit \emph{below}
the correct physics on the data the physics generated.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:limits}

The headline setting runs the calcium conductance at $\gcaMain$, five times the
physiological density, which makes the hidden current easier to see than it would
be in a real cell; at $\gcaPhys$ the closure learns essentially nothing, and the
direct fit still recovers the conductance. Every result is one model, one
trajectory family and one closure architecture, so ``flexibility costs
identifiability'' is demonstrated here rather than established in general. The
matched-objective control swaps the objective but keeps multi-start
Nelder--Mead, so it never isolates the optimiser --- hence the bound rather than
a value in Sec.~\ref{sec:decomp}. A ladder of intermediate closures between two
parameters and a full network, which would locate where identifiability is
actually lost, is the obvious next experiment and is not run here.

Embedding known physics and learning one hidden ionic current makes extrapolation
across the whole remainder of the \Tfull\,ms trajectory accurate and dependable,
and yields a closure that agrees closely with the true calcium current's shape.
It does not yield that current's conductance --- and the reason is not the one
that would be assumed. The single noisy trajectory determines the conductance to
about six per cent, as a direct parametric fit on the very same data shows. It is
the flexible closure that loses it. For simulator-based inference the practical
consequence is narrow and concrete: a UDE that forecasts well, fits inside its
noise floor, and distils to an excellent curve fit can still put its recovered
coefficient anywhere across a factor of two, and none of the diagnostics one
would normally trust --- forecast error, $R^2$, training loss --- gives any
warning. Parametric identifiability of the underlying model does not transfer to
the closure that replaces it.
